# Tales: how Vulcans tell a story

Three short tales in Federation Standard Vulcan, with English, and the storytelling conventions they follow. The Vulcan renders from the dictionary. Canon gives the frame; the texts are design.

## What canon shows

| Source | What it shows |
|---|---|
| VOY "Innocence" | Tuvok sings *Falor's Journey*, "a Vulcan tale of enlightenment" of 348 verses, to calm frightened children; his youngest son asked for it at bedtime. He also tells them that Vulcan parents never shield their children from the truth. |
| SNW "The Sehlat Who Ate Its Tail" | Spock answers Kirk's "the dog who caught the car" with a Vulcan saying about a sehlat that ate its tail. The saying is the compressed form of a tale. |
| TAS "Yesteryear" | The kahs-wan, the maturity ordeal in the desert. Young Spock goes out alone, and the tale does not spare him: I-Chaya, his sehlat, dies. |
| ENT "Carbon Creek" | T'Pol tells her great-grandmother's story at dinner and declines to say whether it is true. A Vulcan storyteller reports; she does not vouch. |

## The tale form

1. **The opening k'en.** A tale begins *ok'ut-pon … k'en*, "long ago, it is told." The teller did not see it, so the reported evidential is the honest mark. One *k'en* at the opening covers the whole telling, and the narration after it is bare. Earth languages with evidentials do the same: Quechua tales run in the reportative *-si*, and Turkish tales open with *-miş*, "it seems, it is said."
2. **No softening.** A teaching tale for small children still ends in pain, hunger or death when the events lead there, because Vulcan parents never shield their children from the truth. The sehlat bites itself; the stranger dies.
3. **Plain events, counted time.** Days are numbered, actions are listed, and repetition does the work that ornament does in Human tales: *Suhk sokzha-un. suhk makha-un. suhk pash-un.*
4. **Evidentials inside the tale still matter.** Characters must say how they know. The sehlat's mistake is a lesson in evidence: it saw its tail (*ho*) and drew the wrong conclusion from it.
5. **The closing question.** The teller ends with *Took'wul nawak. wakli tu liyan?* and never states the moral. The listener answers with *aukh*, so the lesson is the child's own conclusion. This is how a Vulcan child learns logic from a story.
6. **Verse for long tales.** A long tale is sung in numbered verses with a refrain, a few verses a night, and a night verse closes each sitting. That is why *Falor's Journey* can run to 348 verses and still serve as a lullaby.
7. **Tales become sayings.** A known tale shrinks to a phrase that recalls the whole of it, as "the sehlat who ate its tail" does, and as Japanese *mujun*, "spear-shield," recalls the old story of the merchant whose spear pierces everything and whose shield nothing can pierce.

|---|---|
| *took'wul*, *took'wul-kauk-veh*, *airek-took'wul* | story, storyteller, teaching tale |
| *ok'ut-pon* | long ago ("ancient time") |
| *k'een*, *k'een-kir*, *tafuth* | verse, refrain, journey |
| *kash-fakha* | enlightenment ("mind-dawn") |

## 1. The sehlat who ate its tail

A teaching tale for small children (design, built on the canon saying). The saying means one whose own pursuit undoes them.

| | Vulcan | English |
|---|---|---|
| Teller | *Ok'ut-pon sehlat shuhmkhak vauk shal-un k'en.* | Long ago, it is told, there lived a young sehlat. |
| Teller | *Nek tanakh sehlat tewku sok-wizh kosha-un.* | Every day the sehlat saw its own tail. |
| Teller | *Sehlat meen tewku kul vik-un.* | The sehlat thought that the tail was food. |
| Teller | *sehlat tewku-wizh stil-un:* *Tanakh vauk, tanakh yik, tanakh veet.* | The sehlat chased the tail: one day, two days, three days. |
| Teller | *Suhk sokzha-un.* *Suhk makha-un.* *Suhk pash-un.* | It did not eat. It did not drink. It did not rest. |
| Teller | *kak vauk sehlat-wizh koti-un:* *Ghishun tu stil-pil?* | An elder asked the sehlat: "Why are you chasing it?" |
| Sehlat | *Nash-veh tuhkukhuk.* *Kul weellir ho.* | "I am hungry. Food is near. I see it." |
| Teller | *Tanakh ir, sehlat tewku sok-wizh stil-kire-un.* | On the fourth day, the sehlat caught its tail. |
| Teller | *Sehlat tewku-wizh sikak-un.* *Kisuhk t'omyan.* | It bit the tail. The pain was great. |
| Teller | *Sehlat hauthi-un.* *Tewku nifuhr.* *Sala vral.* | The sehlat sat down. Its tail was short. Its stomach was empty. |
| Elder | *Tu lu-wizh pautil-un.* | "You hunted yourself." |
| Teller | *Took'wul nawak.* *Wakli tu liyan?* | The tale ends. What do you conclude? |
| Child | *Sehlat tewku-wizh kosha-un, khe suhk haska-un, aukh.* | The sehlat saw its tail, but did not understand what it saw. I conclude. |

The saying: *sehlat lith tewku sok-wizh sokzha-un*, "the sehlat who ate its tail."

## 2. Falor's Journey (opening verses and the last)

Sung to children at night (design; canon gives only the title, the subject and the number of verses). Each verse is a trial in two clauses and the refrain *Falor sil-pil*, "Falor walks on." The refrain stays in the ongoing present, so the journey is never finished until verse 348.

| | Vulcan | English |
|---|---|---|
| Verse 1 | *Falor suk sok-wizh vilo-un.* *Khakik silukh, kihzhoo silukh.* *Falor sil-pil.* | Falor left his home. The path was long, the night was long. Falor walks on. |
| Verse 2 | *Falor taukek-wizh p'i-lish-un.* *Mazhiv sinish, ekas-var'e vral.* *Falor sil-pil.* | Falor crossed the desert. The sand was hot, the water-skin empty. Falor walks on. |
| Verse 3 | *Falor heya-wizh k'ekthuh-un.* *Nusa feiri, mune-k'oo weellir.* *Falor sil-pil.* | Falor climbed the mountain. The wind was cold, the stars near. Falor walks on. |
| Verse 4 | *Falor neri sok-wizh nizhikh-un.* *Neri-wizh takh-un, kine-un.* *Falor sil-pil.* | Falor met his fear. He looked at it, and set it aside. Falor walks on. |
| Verse 5 | *Falor sharshei-veh-wizh koti-un:* *Fa patuhol?* *sharshei-veh kelet-un:* *Sil.* *Falor sil-pil.* | Falor asked a teacher: "Where is truth?" The teacher answered: "Walk." Falor walks on. |
| Night verse | *Kihzhoo sarlah-un.* *Pash, wan-kam.* *Falor va dungi sil.* | Night has come. Rest, little one. Falor will walk tomorrow. |
| Verse 348 | *Falor sukfi kir-sarlah-un.* *Suk keit'k'ul; Falor suhk keit'k'ul.* *Tafuth nawak.* | Falor returned home. The home was the same; Falor was not. The journey ends. |

## 3. The kahs-wan

Told to children before their own kahs-wan (design). The desert saying it ends on is from `social-life.md`.

| | Vulcan | English |
|---|---|---|
| Teller | *Ok'ut-pon wan vauk kahs-wanfi lish-un k'en.* | Long ago, it is told, a child went out for the kahs-wan. |
| Teller | *Wan ekas-var'e vauk shele-un.* *Kahs-wan vauk-veh.* | She carried one water-skin. The kahs-wan is faced alone. |
| Teller | *Tanakh veet, wan tirein nishaunik-wizh kazh-un.* | On the third day, she found a wounded stranger. |
| Stranger | *Nash-veh kheshokuk.* | "I am thirsty." |
| Teller | *Wan ekas-var'e-wizh takh-un.* *Ekas suhk khrush.* | The child looked at her water-skin. The water was not enough. |
| Teller | *Khe wan tireinfi ekas-wizh vrek-un.* | But she gave water to the stranger. |
| Teller | *Tirein wanfi ekas-kazhuth-wizh sharshei-un.* | The stranger taught her water-finding. |
| Teller | *Tanakh huk, tirein kakif-un.* *Wan sheka-skak-wizh toia-un.* | On the seventh day, the stranger died. The child built a cairn. |
| Teller | *Tanakh vak, wan sukfi kir-sarlah-un.* | On the tenth day, she returned home. |
| Elders | *Kuth tu vauk-veh suni-sar-un?* | "Did you survive alone?" |
| Child | *Sa.* *Nash-veh ekas-wizh tooveeth-un.* *Tirein nash-vehfi ekas-kazhuth-wizh sharshei-un.* | "No. I shared my water. The stranger taught me water-finding." |
| Elders | *Kahs-wan sau sat'mook.* | "Your kahs-wan is complete." |
| Teller | *Took'wul nawak.* *Wakli tu liyan?* | The tale ends. What do you conclude? |
| Listener | *Ekas tooveethik, suni tooveethik, aukh.* | Water shared is life shared. I conclude. |

## In fiction

- A Vulcan who tells a Human a story will often stop at the end and wait. The Human is expected to conclude something.
- *ok'ut-pon k'en* in a Vulcan's mouth tells the reader a tale is coming and that the teller does not vouch for it, as T'Pol does not in "Carbon Creek."
- A Vulcan parent comforting a child sings *Falor's Journey*, not a lullaby about sleep. The tune soothes; the words still teach.
