# The emotive layer: feeling in the grammar, and what Surak did to it

Before Surak, Vulcans were as passionate as any people. Their language carried feeling in its grammar the way Japanese carries politeness: endings and particles that made a sentence tender, scornful, longing, teasing or defiant. Surak's reform did not delete this layer. It pushed it down the registers. Words that were once everyday became intimate, coarse, poetic or archaic, and the plain feeling-words were replaced by understatement.

The layer matters for three reasons:
- **Writing period scenes.** A Classical speaker who uses it sounds natural; a Standard speaker who uses it is out of control, or deliberately intimate.
- **Writing romance and conflict.** The same layer survives between bondmates and in rage.
- **Writing Romulan.** The Romulans left before the reform took hold, so they kept the layer in everyday speech.

All forms are design. They follow canon patterns: the suffixing seen in *-wizh* and *-un*, and canon *kon*, the particle for naming a feeling.

## The emotive inflections

| Form | What it does | Example | Classical | Standard | Romulan |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| -*pi* | endearment on a name or noun | *kekru-pi*, "my heart, dear one" | everyday | **intimate**: only between bondmates | everyday |
| -*tith* | scorn on a noun: "wretched X" | *nirak-tith*, "you wretched fool" | everyday; fond between lovers | **coarse** | everyday |
| -*shith* | feeling carried in the verb: truly, utterly | *shlel-shith*, "love utterly" | everyday | replaced by the adverb *ni*; survives in liturgy and in anger | everyday |
| -*kuhl* | longing: "would that I could" | *sil-kuhl*, "long to walk" | everyday | **poetic, archaic** | everyday |
| *sheil* | sentence-final tag: "is it not so?" | *tu t'ithton, sheil*, "you're stubborn, aren't you" | everyday | **colloquial**: teasing, argument | everyday |
| *vash* | sentence-final challenge: "prove it" | *shlel-sefik paus, vash*, "sing a love song, I dare you" | everyday | **coarse** | everyday (*Fve'hna!*) |
| *yo* | sentence-final coaxing: "won't you?" | *… sil-kuhl, yo* | everyday; the particle of courtship | **intimate** | everyday |
| *le* | exclamation of feeling | *Le!* | everyday | suppressed, except under strain | everyday |

The pattern behind the fates: whatever performs a feeling (endearment, scorn, emphasis, longing) went down to intimate, coarse or archaic. Whatever only names a feeling survived, because naming is logical. That is why *kon*, "noted," is the one emotion particle Standard Vulcan uses freely.

## Using the layer in fiction

- **Classical scenes:** use the inflections freely. A suitor says *kekru-pi*; an enemy says *nirak-tith*.
- **Standard scenes:** use the reserved column. A Vulcan who says *nash-veh tu-wizh pahk-tor* has said "I love you", and everyone present knows it.
- **Breaking reserve:** a Standard speaker who reaches for the old layer shows the strain. Use *le*, -*shith* in anger, or -*pi* in private, under pon farr, or at a deathbed. One old ending is enough.
- **Romulan:** the layer is ordinary speech. *aehn'ru* "darling," *mnaelh* "court openly," *khrrhae* "quarrel loudly," *Fve'hna!* "I dare you!" and *llhoi*, a fond insult. To a Vulcan ear, an ordinary Romulan sounds like a Classical poet in a temper.
