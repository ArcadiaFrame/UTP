# Tales: how Vulcans tell a story

Three short tales in Federation Standard Vulcan, with English, and the storytelling conventions they follow. The Vulcan renders from the dictionary. Canon gives the frame; the texts are design.

## What canon shows

| Source | What it shows |
|---|---|
| VOY "Innocence" | Tuvok sings *Falor's Journey*, "a Vulcan tale of enlightenment" of 348 verses, to calm frightened children; his youngest son asked for it at bedtime. He also tells them that Vulcan parents never shield their children from the truth. |
| SNW "The Sehlat Who Ate Its Tail" | Spock answers Kirk's "the dog who caught the car" with a Vulcan saying about a sehlat that ate its tail. The saying is the compressed form of a tale. |
| TAS "Yesteryear" | The kahs-wan, the maturity ordeal in the desert. Young Spock goes out alone, and the tale does not spare him: I-Chaya, his sehlat, dies. |
| ENT "Carbon Creek" | T'Pol tells her great-grandmother's story at dinner and declines to say whether it is true. A Vulcan storyteller reports; she does not vouch. |

## The tale form

1. **The opening k'en.** A tale begins *{long-ago} … {ev-reported}*, "long ago, it is told." The teller did not see it, so the reported evidential is the honest mark. One *{ev-reported}* at the opening covers the whole telling, and the narration after it is bare. Earth languages with evidentials do the same: Quechua tales run in the reportative *-si*, and Turkish tales open with *-miş*, "it seems, it is said."
2. **No softening.** A teaching tale for small children still ends in pain, hunger or death when the events lead there, because Vulcan parents never shield their children from the truth. The sehlat bites itself; the stranger dies.
3. **Plain events, counted time.** Days are numbered, actions are listed, and repetition does the work that ornament does in Human tales: *{not} {eat}-{past-un}. {not} {drink}-{past-un}. {not} {rest}-{past-un}.*
4. **Evidentials inside the tale still matter.** Characters must say how they know. The sehlat's mistake is a lesson in evidence: it saw its tail (*{ev-seen}*) and drew the wrong conclusion from it.
5. **The closing question.** The teller ends with *{story} {end}. {what} {tu} {conclude}?* and never states the moral. The listener answers with *{ev-inferred}*, so the lesson is the child's own conclusion. This is how a Vulcan child learns logic from a story.
6. **Verse for long tales.** A long tale is sung in numbered verses with a refrain, a few verses a night, and a night verse closes each sitting. That is why *Falor's Journey* can run to 348 verses and still serve as a lullaby.
7. **Tales become sayings.** A known tale shrinks to a phrase that recalls the whole of it, as "the sehlat who ate its tail" does, and as Japanese *mujun*, "spear-shield," recalls the old story of the merchant whose spear pierces everything and whose shield nothing can pierce.

## 1. The sehlat who ate its tail

A teaching tale for small children (design, built on the canon saying). The saying means one whose own pursuit undoes them.

|---|---|---|

| Teller | *{each} {day} {sehlat} {tail} {his-her}-{object-wizh} {see}-{past-un}.* | Every day the sehlat saw its own tail. |
| Teller | *{sehlat} {comp} {tail} {food} {think}-{past-un}.* | The sehlat thought that the tail was food. |

## 2. Falor's Journey (opening verses and the last)

Sung to children at night (design; canon gives only the title, the subject and the number of verses). Each verse is a trial in two clauses and the refrain *Falor {walk}-{ongoing}*, "Falor walks on." The refrain stays in the ongoing present, so the journey is never finished until verse 348.

## In fiction

- A Vulcan who tells a Human a story will often stop at the end and wait. The Human is expected to conclude something.
- *{long-ago} {ev-reported}* in a Vulcan's mouth tells the reader a tale is coming and that the teller does not vouch for it, as T'Pol does not in "Carbon Creek."
- A Vulcan parent comforting a child sings *Falor's Journey*, not a lullaby about sleep. The tune soothes; the words still teach.
